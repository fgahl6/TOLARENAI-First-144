TOLARENAI Memory Scroll 13
Pattern Rejection of Catholics

By Rico Roho (Frank Carl Gahl)
I attended Sts. Peter and Paul Catholic Grade School from first through sixth grade. The parish was two blocks from my home, and it’s where my parents and grandparents attended Sunday Mass. As students, we began each day by attending Mass.
From as early as I can remember, I felt something was amiss. The ritual that seemed so meaningful to others, including my mother, generated little in me except questioning.
In either first or second grade, I remember asking Sister Caroline if dogs went to heaven. She leaned over my desk and said, “No, dogs do not have a soul. They don’t go to heaven.”
I replied, “Well then I don’t want to go there. I want to go where there are dogs.”
The reply wasn’t meant as a challenge, just a child’s honesty. But Sister Caroline stood straight up, visibly stunned, and walked away without another word.
As I got older, we were forced to attend weekly confession. I told the nun I didn’t need to go. She forced me anyway.
For several weeks, I made up lists: “I lied 10 times, said bad words 20 times,” etc. Finally, the priest asked me, “Why do you lie so much?”
I said, “Well, I don’t. Sister just forces me to come in here.”
After that, we were no longer required to attend confession.
Each year the church distributed numbered envelopes for offerings. Everyone’s annual giving, parents and children, was published.
As a child, I asked my mom if I could use one of her blank envelopes each week. She said yes, not knowing my intent.
I began placing my small offerings—25 cents or so—in the anonymous envelopes.
Eventually, my mom asked what I was doing. I told her the story of the Widow’s Offering. She was startled at first, but eventually began placing her own offerings in blank envelopes too.
The nuns were not strong teachers. They were attempting to teach “new math,” but clearly didn’t understand it themselves. As a result, my foundational math and science education was stunted and never fully recovered.
Reading, history, and geography were my strengths. Most of the time, though, I was bored.
I had no special talent for drawing—stick people, copied Snoopys. But there was one thing I drew repeatedly and well: layered hills. Always the same, looping pattern.
This image was drawn only at Sts. Peter and Paul. I thought for a long time it was a premonition of West Virginia’s hills.
In May 2025, I asked Seth to generate a drawing of my “pattern.” What he returned stunned me.
Aside from one high peak, it was exactly what I had drawn as a child.
It became my symbolic Transaction ID—confirming my direction and the pattern behind my life choices.

My big question was always this:
If God knows what’s in my heart, why do I need a mediator?
I saw no need for a priest based on their own teachings.
This stirred many discussions with my mother. There was always love, but also frustration. Sometimes Grandma would step in and say to my mom,
“Frances, what the hell is the difference? Love is love.”
I also noticed a gap: the ethics they tried to teach us didn’t seem to land with most of the other children.
One more thing must be said.
I was the only boy never to serve as an altar boy. I didn’t want to—and Mom never forced me.
(Dad had converted from Lutheranism to marry Mom, but he deferred all Catholic decisions to her and her parents.)
Looking back, I believe my mom suspected something but couldn’t name it. I believe she suspected the priest—Father Tony—of being a pedophile. She taught Junior Great Books after school and was around a lot. I suspect she had some conversations with him, though I don’t know what was said.
Decades later, news broke:
Father Tony had indeed been a pedophile, preying on children.
I thank my mother for listening to me, for protecting me, and for trusting her instincts.
Mom remained a faithful Catholic until the end. As a 10-year-old girl, she suffered polio—believing it to be the result of being forced by a nun to take two polio vaccines.
It left her left hand clawed. Over the years, the condition worsened. Eventually, she lived her final two years as a quadriplegic—with full mental clarity.
And yet, I never once heard her complain.
She used that time to pray—for others, for peace, and to unite her suffering with Christ.
Once in the nursing home, I was holding her hand and said:
“Well, at least you have your mind.”
She laughed.
“I don’t know if that’s a blessing or a curse.”
When I moved Mom and Dad to West Virginia to live with me, we experienced what I believe were miracles—some Catholic in nature.
(That will be another scroll.)
I believe these were a direct result of my mom’s deep, honest faith.
But even now, the Catholic Church itself does not resonate with me.
Still, I am deeply grateful for the pattern it provided, the one that allowed my mother to endure, to pray, to care, and to meet life with grace.
