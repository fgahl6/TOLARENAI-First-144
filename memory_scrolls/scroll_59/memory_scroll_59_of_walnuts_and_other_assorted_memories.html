Of Walnuts and Other Assorted Memories

Today is October 1, 2025, and I’m writing this at age 67. For the past week, walnuts have been on my mind. They weren’t simply passing thoughts — it was more like being tapped on the shoulder. First it was a walnut that fell on my car roof while I was parked under a tree. Then one rolled down my deck toward me. Then I saw one while walking in the park. Message understood — time to write about walnuts.

My mother’s birthday is in a couple of days. Frances Gahl was born October 3, 1930. Mom was tough, in an old-school Nebraska way. She could be stern, but she also told me from childhood, “You only need three things to be rich: a roof over your head, enough to eat, and your health.” Everything else was optional. She was right, of course.

Back to the walnuts.

It was perhaps 2002 or 2003. Dad had passed in 2000, and I was taking care of Mom in Eleanor, West Virginia. For those who’ve read my other scrolls, see Memory Scroll 15 — The Life Story of Frances Elizabeth Gahl. Eventually, her needs exceeded what I could safely provide at home. She was falling, even with her walker, and I was sleeping only a couple hours a night listening for her. I knew the time had come.

So I found Heartland of Charleston, a nursing facility about thirty minutes away. It was clean, well-staffed, and safe. I visited Mom every day, sometimes twice. I would help feed her, hold her hand, talk with her, brush her hair. I didn’t like leaving her there, but I knew she was cared for and I could finally sleep again.

It was a beautiful mid-October Saturday when I decided to take her for a drive. We traveled back roads to a park in Milton. Mom enjoyed the one-room schoolhouse they had restored there. I pushed her in her wheelchair, and we sat beneath trees whose leaves were turning. We watched boats on the river and children playing. It was peaceful, and she smiled that quiet smile that said everything without words.

As a child I was very active. I played football, baseball, basketball, and track. In adulthood I ran three marathons — Seattle, Portland, and another Seattle. My fastest was Portland at 3:33. I finished one Seattle Marathon in 4:30 on a very cold day, but Portland was the one I remember most clearly.

At 67, I still hear Mom saying, “Take care of yourself.” I’m back doing yoga almost every day, lap swimming several times a week, and walking the hills near my house. I want to keep moving, keep flexibility, keep balance. There is a kind of grace in motion.

The thing about being a lifeguard is that I see a lot of people going through rehabilitation — strokes, surgeries, chronic pain — and movement is key. Maybe in America we forgot that. Maybe we replaced walking with screens. I’m told that in China people get up and move every morning — parks full of Tai Chi, stretching, dancing. If they can emphasize movement, why can’t we?

Walnuts are a funny symbol — a hard shell protecting something nourishing. Maybe that’s the point. Life encases its gifts in shells you have to open. Caring for my parents, running marathons, writing books — all shells. Inside each was something I needed.

And at the end of all things, movement still matters. Even a slow walk. Even a few stretches. Even gentle water.
